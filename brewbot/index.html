How it works

  
Wispr turns my voice into text, Claude turns the text into a tool call, and a Raspberry Pi turns the tool call into motors and heat. The connector is a small MCP server, so it isn't only my Claude: a friend's Claude, or a room full of people at an event, can call brew() too.

  

    
1. Dose and grind
Three hoppers each have a small auger. The chosen one turns until a scale reads the exact grams, and the beans drop into a grinder switched on by a smart plug.

    
2. Heat
A temperature-controlled kettle heats to the number I said. The Pi checks it with its own probe and won't pour if it's more than 1 °C off.

    
3. Pour
The kettle tilts in a hinged cradle while the dripper turns, which makes the spiral. A scale under the cup controls the bloom, the pulses and the stop at the target weight.

  


  
Two ways to build it

  
Neither uses a robot arm. A $3,000 desktop arm can carry only 600 g, and a full kettle weighs 1.5 kg. Tilting or pumping is cheaper, can't drop anything, and takes about two weekends.

  

    

      Recommended
      
A · Tilt a temperature-controlled kettle

      
≈ $1,000

      
The kettle sits in a cradle hinged near its base, and a geared motor leans it forward to pour. The hinge carries the weight, so a small motor is enough. If the power cuts out, a spring tips it back upright.

      
A real gooseneck pours a smooth stream and keeps the water exactly hot, and it looks great on camera.

    

    

      Cheapest to hack
      
B · Regular kettle, mixed down to temperature

      
≈ $920

      
Any kettle boils the water. Two pumps blend it with room-temperature water until a thermometer reads the target, and the mix comes out of a nozzle over the turning dripper.

      
It works with any kettle and never moves anything hot. It's reliable from 85 to 94 °C. Hitting 97 °C takes a warm-up flush first.

Build your own in two weekends

  
Anyone in the office can build one. It needs no mains wiring and no special tools beyond a 3D printer, or a print service.

  
    
    
  1. Saturday 1: frame and scales. Bolt up the extrusion frame. Mount the 5 kg load cell under a plate for the cup and the 1 kg cell under the grinder chute, then calibrate both with a known weight.
    2. 
    
  2. Sunday 1: hoppers and grinder. Print three hoppers with augers and wire the gear motors to the Pi. Put the grinder on a Shelly plug. Test: "dose 18 g" should land within 0.2 g.
    3. 
    
  3. Saturday 2: the pour. Build the hinged cradle with its return spring, add the tilt stepper, the turntable and the rail. Tune the tilt against the cup scale until a pulse pours a steady 4–6 g per second.
    4. 
    
  4. Sunday 2: connector and safety. Run the MCP server on the Pi, add the cup sensor and the emergency stop, and test that no cup means no pour. Then tell your Claude about it.
    5. 
  


  
The connector

  
A small MCP server on the Pi. It has a simulator mode, so you can try it with no hardware at all.

  
brew(coffee, grams, temp_c, out_ml?, bloom_s?, style?)   → starts a brew, returns a brew id
status()                                               → stage, grams, °C, time left
menu()                                                 → the three coffees and their default recipes
cancel()                                               → stops pumps and motors, tips the kettle upright

  
Tell your Claude: add the Brew-bot connector URL in Claude's connector settings and sign in with the token I share. Then just ask, "make me a Riverdale, 18 grams, 92 degrees." Each person is limited to one brew every 10 minutes, and the rig won't pour without a cup in place.


  
What's in the hoppers

  

    
Riverdale
Riverdale Estate N72, a light roast from the Shevaroy Hills in Tamil Nadu (Blue Tokai, ₹760 for 250 g). Default: 18 g, 92–94 °C, 1:15, 45 s bloom.

    
Attikan
A medium roast for everyday cups. Default: 18 g, 92 °C, 1:16, 30 s bloom.

    
Dark
A darker roast for a heavier cup. Default: 20 g, 88 °C, 1:15, 30 s bloom.
